Read a raster in SQL: the value under a point with ST_Value, the cells inside a polygon with ST_Clip and ST_ValueCount, and areas from cell counts, with the error of the method that made the published habitat summaries. Everything runs in your own PostgreSQL with PostGIS, on the lab tables (the setup page).

Concept

How to read a raster in SQL, and how to turn its cells into an area you can defend:

  • A raster is a grid of cells, each holding a number. Six numbers place it on the Earth, its georeference: the x and y of its upper-left corner, the cell's width and height (the height negative, since rows run south), and two skews (0 here). With the SRID they say where every cell is; ST_MetaData returns them. In PostGIS a raster is a value of type raster in a column. It can hold its cells in the database, as here, or point at a file outside it (out-db), which needs two settings and GDAL drivers a server doesn't enable by default.
  • The habitat map (Jung et al. 2020, v004, CC BY 4.0, doi 10.5281/zenodo.4058819) gives each cell a class of the IUCN habitat classification scheme, written as a number: 106 is class 1.6, "Forest – Subtropical/tropical moist lowland", and 1401 is 14.1, "Arable land". The 100 m map's grid is longitude–latitude, SRID 4326, with cells 0.0008983152841195215° on a side: 100 m at the equator, narrower east–west by cos φ away from it (Coordinate reference systems, g1). It isn't Web Mercator, and no cell is 100 m by 100 m except on the equator.
  • The value under a point: ST_Value(rast, band, point) returns the value of the cell holding the point, and ST_Intersects(rast, point) says whether the point is on the raster at all (ArcGIS Pro's Extract Values to Points).
  • The cells inside a polygon: ST_Clip(rast, polygon) keeps the cells whose centre is inside the polygon and sets the rest to nodata (Extract by Mask). ST_ValueCount counts cells by value, leaving nodata out (Tabulate Area, in cells). ST_PixelAsCentroids returns each cell as a row: its value, column x, row y and centre point, so SQL can do the rest.
  • From cells to area. A count of cells becomes an area once each cell has its ground area. On a longitude–latitude grid every cell in a row has the same area, so one geodesic area per row, ST_Area(ST_PixelAsPolygon(rast, 1, row)::geography), times the row's count is exact for the grid. Cells × (100 m)² overstates by the cos φ shrink. Even exact for the grid, the result is an estimate: the map is a model's classification, and the outline picks cells by their centres.
  • Two ways to summarise a country. Method 1 counts the 100 m cells whose centre is inside the outline, each at its row's area. Method 3, which critwire publishes as country_habitats, reads the map's 1 km fractions instead (for each class, the per mille of each 1 km cell it covers) and sums fraction × cell area over the 1 km cells whose centre is inside. The two differ in the border cells: a 1 km cell the outline crosses counts whole or not at all, by its centre, so classes along a coast carry the largest errors.

Data

habitat_clips, two windows of the 100 m map stored as in-database rasters of 836 × 836 cells: cr, longitude −84.20…−83.45 and latitude 9.85…10.60, from San José to the Caribbean lowlands; tas, 147.25…148.00 and −43.10…−42.35, south-east Tasmania with Hobart (the lab data). Each window is the 0.75° square holding the most of the sample's records for its country: whole countries at 100 m would take about 70 MB. Also habitat_classes, country_habitats (method 3, every country), occurrence_habitats (each record's 1 km class), country_shapes and occurrences. The rasters are in the database, so no raster setting is needed: every block runs on a server's defaults.

Records gather where people go to look, so these counts describe the recording as much as the animals; Zonal statistics (s6, in preparation) compares records with the classes' areas.

  4. A record's 100 m class against its 1 km class. occurrence_habitats gives each record the class with the largest fraction in its 1 km cell. Build that cell from the 1 km grid's numbers (upper-left corner −180.0099155291168, 90.00297126602864; cells 0.009915667822884942° on a side), and find the most common 100 m class inside it:

    {{block:g7-scale}}

    The record's own 100 m cell has its 1 km class for 49 of the 202 records in Costa Rica (24 %) and 332 of 601 in Tasmania (55 %). Yet the 1 km class is the most common 100 m class in its cell for 201 and 579: the two maps agree; most records just aren't on the majority. Only 50 of the Costa Rican records are; 77 sit on montane forest in 1 km cells that are mostly arable land. The 23 records whose 1 km class isn't the 100 m majority are all in cells where two classes tie or nearly tie (20 Tasmanian records share one cell with 48 cells each of temperate forest and pastureland), except one in a cell cut by the window's edge. A 1 km class describes the landscape around a record, a 100 m class the patch it is on; neither is the species' habitat.

  5. How good are the published summaries? Method 3 summed over each country, against the outline's area on the ellipsoid, in bands of error:

    {{block:g7-countries}}

    Over all 247 outlines the estimate is 0.999074 of the outlines' area (−0.09 %), the figure critwire's ingest records with the files. 215 countries are within 1 %, every outline over 431 km² among them. The error grows as the outline shrinks, since a small island holds few 1 km cells and each counts whole or not at all: Vatican City (0.71 km²) holds no cell centre and has no rows; Tuvalu's atolls, 10.21 km², get 13.16 km² (+28.9 %); Tokelau −28.6 %, Sint Maarten −17.3 %. Read a small country's habitat areas as rough.

Result

A cell of v004's 100 m grid covers 9,768 to 9,790 m² in the Costa Rica window and 7,298 to 7,386 m² in Tasmania's; counting cells as 100 m squares overstates by 2.3 % and 36 %. Costa Rica's window holds 202 records and an estimated 6,785.90 km² of land inside the outline, 36.5 % of it arable; its records sit mostly on forest (87 montane, 30 lowland). A record's 100 m class matches its 1 km class for 24 % (Costa Rica) and 55 % (Tasmania) of records, although the 1 km class is the 100 m majority around all but 23 of the 803. The published summaries total 0.999074 of the outlines' area; 215 of 247 countries are within 1 %, the worst are islands under 135 km².

Check

The answer: assign the cells, count each 1 km cell's centres with a window function, and sum both ways side by side. The share of each class's method 1 area that lies in 1 km cells the outline cuts tells you why:

Over the window's land the methods differ by 0.2 %: 3,944.68 km² by method 3 against 3,953.67 km² by method 1. The classes that differ most, by 1.1 to 2.8 %, are intertidal (1200), urban areas (1405), inland wetlands (500), marine neritic (900) and seagrass (909): 5 to 15 % of their area lies in 1 km cells the outline crosses, where method 3 takes the whole cell or none of it. Classes away from the coast agree within 0.4 %, and what's left there is the grids' misfit: a 1 km cell spans 11.04 cells of 100 m, so a whole one holds 121, 132 or 144 of their centres, and sharing its area among them is close, not exact. An earlier comparison over all of Trinidad and Tobago and of Åland, on 1:10m outlines, found the same pattern, larger: marine classes +12.5 % and +13.9 %, with three quarters of their area in border cells. The comparison judges the summary's method, not the map: both columns are estimates from the same classification.

Dialects

  • PostgreSQL: PostGIS's raster type comes in the postgis_raster extension. raster2pgsql loads a file in-db, or out-db with -R, which then needs postgis.enable_outdb_rasters and postgis.gdal_enabled_drivers. ST_SummaryStats, ST_Reclass and ST_MapAlgebra compute over cells; ST_Tile cuts a large raster into rows of a table.
  • DuckDB: core DuckDB has no raster type. Read a raster's cells outside it (rasterio, as critwire's ingest does) into rows of value, column and row, then count them in SQL.
  • SQL Server: no raster type either. Store cells as rows (column, row, value) or one blob decoded outside, and summarise with joins and GROUP BY; zonal counts then follow this module's SQL with the cell's polygon built from the grid's numbers.
